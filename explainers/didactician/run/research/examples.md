# Examples

### x-01
objective: o-3
example: Reading the word "characteristics": for a fluent reader it is one element pulled from long-term memory; for someone learning to read English it is many interacting squiggles that can overwhelm working memory.
why: Shows that load depends on the reader, not just the material, which sets up why the brief fixes one audience. Breaks down if read as "experts have bigger working memory": they have bigger chunks.
source: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5 (Sweller et al. 2019, "For readers of this paper, the English word 'characteristics' … is processed … as a single element")

### x-02
objective: o-3
example: A geometry diagram with its solution steps printed in a separate block forces the reader to match each statement to the figure; integrating the steps onto the diagram removes that work. Web equivalent: a chart legend versus labels placed directly on each line.
why: Makes split attention (c-10) concrete for a web reader, and maps straight onto Didactician's default of direct labels over legends (DESIGN.md#L438).
source: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5 (split-attention section; Tarmizi & Sweller 1988 origin)

### x-03
objective: o-3
example: Expertise reversal: step-by-step worked examples and integrated explanations that help novices "can be counterproductive as expertise increases", because experts must cross-check the guidance against what they already know. For this page's readers (fluent in agents, new to learning science) agent mechanics can be brisk while cognitive-load concepts need scaffolding.
why: Lets the reader predict that one design choice raises load for one audience and lowers it for another, and explains why the skill designs for one stated audience with optional collapsible scaffolding.
source: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5 ("Instructional procedures designed for novices dealing with multiple, interacting elements can be counterproductive as expertise increases")
