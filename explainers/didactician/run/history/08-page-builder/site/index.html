How Didactician Teaches

❧

Being an Account of a Machine that Plans Its Pages as Lessons

About 10 minutesfor Claude Code usersmade with Didactician

Didactician is a Claude Code skill that turns a request like “explain X to Y” into a static explainer page. It plans every page as a lesson: who the reader is, what they should be able to do afterwards, and what to leave out.

§ 1.This page comes in two halves. The first shows the machinery: eight stages, each handing the next a file, and the reasons the pipeline is built the way it is. The second opens the stage that makes the output teach rather than merely inform, and the one idea from learning science that stage rests on, cognitive load.

§ 2 The Pipeline, One Stage at a Time

The whole apparatus

Scroll on: each step opens one stage, and the plate stays in view.

Eight stages take you from a one-line request to a folder you can deploy. Each stage hands the next one a file, never a chat message, so every step leaves behind something you can open and read.

The shape of a stage tells you who does the work: rectangles are agents, the hexagon is a script, and the rounded stages are where you come in.

Stage 1 · Intake brief.md

You type one line. Your own Claude Code session acts as the orchestrator and turns it into a brief: who the reader is, how long they have, and what they should be able to do afterwards.

It asks you a question only when it can’t infer the answer. For this page it asked none.

Stage 2 · Research facts.md

A researcher answers the brief’s questions and stops at a cap. Each claim it keeps is tied to an objective and goes into the fact base with a source, a quote and a confidence.

Things that are true but not needed go to a parking lot, and the raw reading never enters the context that writes the page.

Stage 3 · Plan learning-plan.md

An instructional designer turns the brief and the facts into a learning plan: what each section is for, how it’s laid out, and where the reader is asked to think. It also draws a wireframe mockup and writes a cut list of what it left out.

This is the stage that makes the output teach. § 5 opens it up, using this page’s own plan.

Stage 4 · You approve decisions.md

Always, and before anything is built. You open the mockup, read the objectives and the cut list, then approve, revise or rescope.

Your answer is written down as a decision that later stages must follow. For this page, the user asked for one change and approved the rest.

Stage 5 · Build site/

A page builder writes the page: the prose, the figures and the exercises. Each complex widget, like the plate beside this text, gets a builder of its own.

All of them work from the approved plan and the fact base, not from a conversation.

Stage 6 · Gate page-round-N.json

A script, verify.mjs, opens the page in a real browser through Playwright at 360, 768 and 1280 pixels wide, and fails it on anything a machine can check.

A failure sends the page back to the builder, for a limited number of rounds.

Stage 7 · Review findings.md

Two reviewers, one for accuracy and one for teaching and UX, each start in a fresh context. They see the brief, the fact base and the page, and never the builder’s report.

Each finding gets an ID, and blocking ones go back to the builder before anything ships.

Stage 8 · Deliver site/ + log.md

You get site/, a static folder ready for any static host. Beside it sits run/, the record of the whole run, starting with log.md.

§ 3 Why It’s Built This Way

None of those stages is unusual on its own. What matters is the shape, and each part of it exists to prevent a failure you have probably met in your own agent work. Start with checking, because a page that teaches something false is worse than no page at all.

Problem 1 · Predict

The page builder has finished the page. Before it ships, which check would you trust most to catch a claim it got subtly wrong?

Pick one to see the solution.

Solution.B. Option A is cheap and tempting, but the builder knows what it meant and reads that intent back into the text. Studies of LLM judges also find they rate their own outputs higher than human annotators do (shown on GPT-4 and Llama 2).

C only checks what a machine can check, such as overflow, broken arrowheads and console errors, never whether a sentence is true. Didactician runs C, then B. Whether that split beats one agent reviewing itself hasn’t been measured; the skill’s own design notes list it as a hypothesis to test.

That answer is one of four rules that shape the pipeline. The plate below is the same apparatus, with each rule drawn onto the part it governs.

Rule 1 · The orchestrator never authors

Your main session dispatches agents and records what they return. It never writes the content, the plan, the site code or a review verdict.

So no reviewer is grading work that its own context produced, and the raw research stays out of the context that writes the prose.

Rule 2 · Everything is a file

Stages hand each other files, never chat. Each agent’s prompt and its report are saved as a pair under run/handoffs/.

Afterwards you can read exactly what every agent was told and what it said back.

Rule 3 · Scripts gate what’s checkable

Four scripts sit between stages and can fail them. A fifth, round_gate.py, must approve every dispatch, and it refuses when a cap is reached, when a loop repeats the same failure, or when the last report is missing. That’s why loops can’t run away.

What no script can settle, such as the page’s shape, whether it needs exercises, or a table of contents, is left to agents, who must state a reason, and to the reviewers who judge it. Accuracy is one of those: no machine can check it, so it is enforced through review.

Rule 4 · You approve before anything expensive

The skill’s design notes call a wrong premise in the plan the most expensive error in the system, since every later stage builds on it.

So the checkpoint is the premise check. It comes while the page is still a mockup, before any building, gating or reviewing has been paid for.

§ 4 The Idea Underneath: Cognitive Load

Read this word: characteristics.

You took that word in at a glance, as one thing. Someone learning to read English meets fifteen letters that have to be worked through and combined, and that can be more than they can hold at once.

The word is the same for both readers; what differs is what each one brings to it. Cognitive load theory explains the difference with one premise. New information has to pass through a working memory that holds only a little, and only for a short time. What you have already learned lives in long-term memory, and when you use it, it comes back as a single chunk that escapes those limits (Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas, 2019). An expert doesn’t have a bigger working memory. They have bigger chunks.

Cognitive load is how much of that working memory a page demands, and it comes from two sources.

Intrinsic load
comes from the material: how many elements have to be held together at once, relative to what the reader already knows. Better presentation can’t remove it. It changes only when you change what is learned at one time, for instance by teaching the parts before the whole, or when the reader’s expertise grows.
Extraneous load
comes from the presentation, and from what the page makes the reader do. It adds nothing to learning, and it is the part a designer controls. Most of the effects that researchers report come from varying this kind of load.

Here is some extraneous load you can feel. Both versions of the plate below hold the same information about the pipeline you’ve just seen. Answer the question above them using each version in turn.

That is the split-attention effect. When readers have to integrate a figure with text placed somewhere else, load goes up and learning suffers; putting the text on the figure removes the effect (Sweller et al., 2019). It’s why Didactician’s figures label the parts directly instead of using a legend, and why every plate on this page does the same.

Problem 2 · Predict

This page never explained what a subagent or a context window is. For you, would adding a step-by-step explanation of them have raised or lowered your cognitive load?

Pick one to see the solution.

Solution.B. To you, “subagent” is already one chunk. Walking through it piece by piece gives you nothing to learn, but you still have to process it and cross-check it against what you know, and that is extraneous load.

For a reader new to agents, the same paragraphs would lower load. This is the expertise reversal effect: guidance that helps novices can be counterproductive as expertise grows. That’s why Didactician fixes one audience per page, and why this page moves quickly through the agent material and slowly through the learning science.

Why not a third, “good” kind of load?

Older accounts of the theory added a third kind, germane load: the effort that builds understanding, which instruction should increase. Critics argued that a theory with a good, a bad and a neutral load can explain any result after the fact, which makes it hard to falsify (de Jong, 2010). The theory’s authors now describe germane load as working memory redirected from extraneous activity toward the content, rather than an extra quantity added to the total (Sweller et al., 2019).

So Didactician doesn’t try to add “good” effort. It removes friction from the presentation and points the attention that frees up at the content.

§ 5 Stage 3: Where Load Becomes Page Decisions

§ 4 was planned before it was written. At stage 3 the instructional designer wrote an entry for it in this page’s learning plan, and the user approved that entry at the checkpoint. Step through it below. Each highlighted line is a decision, and each decision produced a part of § 4 that you have just read or answered.

Notice what that plan didn’t ask for: a widget in § 4. It’s tempting to believe that interactive means engaging, and engaging means effective, so that anything a reader can drag must teach. Try that belief on a slider.

Problem 3 · Compare

A widget lets the reader drag a slider and watch a value change. Which change does most to make it teach?

Pick one to see the solution.

Solution.B. Dragging is Active: the reader does something but doesn’t have to think. Predicting first makes it Constructive, because the reader generates something that isn’t on the screen, and a wrong guess followed by the real result is what sticks (the ICAP framework, which its authors frame as a hypothesis).

A is good UX but doesn’t change what the reader thinks about. C adds interacting elements, so it adds load. Didactician’s designer is told to put Constructive moments at the key ideas and known misconceptions, not in every section. That’s why this page asks three questions, not one per section.

The designer also sizes a page’s scope in ideas, by how many fit the reader’s time, and lists what it left out in a cut list that you see at the checkpoint, before anything is built.

§ 6 What You Get

You ask once and approve a plan once. You get back a folder you can deploy, next to a folder that records how it was made.

  1. Intake
  2. Research
  3. Plan
  4. You approve
  5. Build
  6. Gate
  7. Review
  8. Deliver

Here is that second folder, for this page. Start at run/log.md. It is a timeline of the run, and each entry links the files it refers to: the brief, the user’s decisions, and every agent’s prompt and report.

Two limits are worth knowing before you install. Nobody has yet measured whether the separate reviewers catch more than one agent checking its own work; the skill’s design notes list that comparison as a test still to run. And the caps hold only as long as the orchestrator follows the skill’s instructions: a skipped gate call would show up in the log, but nothing makes it impossible.
